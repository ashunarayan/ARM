% --- ABSTRACT ---
\begin{abstract}
Continuous monitoring of urban pavement roughness is critical for infrastructure preservation, yet Class~1 laser profilometers remain cost-prohibitive ($>\$50{,}000$ per vehicle). While crowdsourced smartphone inertial sensing offers a scalable alternative, existing models suffer from single-vehicle empirical overfitting and speed-dependent signal distortion ($a_z \propto v^2$). We present a speed-invariant, multi-vehicle International Roughness Index (IRI) estimation architecture trained via a Sim-to-Real (Sim2Real) soft-body physics pipeline. Leveraging BeamNG.tech across three distinct vehicle classes and urban/rural environments, we formulate a Curvature-Adaptive Mesh Tessellation Correction to resolve artificial polygon-facet slope oscillations and generate standardized quarter-car ground truth. The resulting synthetic corpus trains a lightweight Dual-Branch Depthwise-Separable 1D-CNN incorporating kinetic normalization, 2-D inverse-frequency sampling, Asymmetric Huber Loss, and Post-Training Isotonic Calibration (PICA). On a multi-vehicle validation benchmark, our model achieves a Mean Absolute Error of $1.642\,\text{m/km}$ ($R^2 = 0.493$), outperforming gradient-boosted trees by 19.1\% and sequential recurrent networks by 30.9\%. Evaluated against thresholds inspired by AASHTO R~56 compliance tiers, the model achieves 77.9\% compliance at municipal screening thresholds ($\pm 1.5\,\text{m/km}$). Zero-shot evaluation on the real-world crowdsourced PVS dataset ($693\,\text{km}$) confirms cross-domain transferability, establishing a deployable edge-inference engine for participatory road monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{The Global Pavement Monitoring Gap.}
Road pavement infrastructure represents one of the most capital-intensive physical assets managed by municipal governments worldwide. In extensive national networks such as India's 6.3~million-kilometer roadway system~\cite{1}, routine structural condition assessment on secondary and tertiary roads occurs infrequently—often spanning intervals of three to five years~\cite{1}. Globally, pavement deterioration inflicts severe economic burdens: the World Bank estimates that poor road conditions cost vehicle owners more than \$500~billion annually through accelerated tire wear, drivetrain damage, fuel overconsumption, and road accident externalities~\cite{2}.

The internationally established metric for quantifying pavement roughness is the International Roughness Index (IRI), standardized under ISO~8608 and ASTM~E1926~\cite{3}. IRI measures the cumulative vertical suspension displacement of a standardized quarter-car traversing a road profile at a reference velocity of $80\,\text{km/h}$, expressed in m/km. While Class~1 laser profilometers achieve sub-millimeter vertical precision, their capital expenditure ($>\$50{,}000$--$\$200{,}000$ per vehicle) renders city-scale continuous monitoring economically intractable.

\textbf{Smartphone Participatory Sensing and Empirical Limitations.}
With over 6.8~billion active devices globally, modern smartphones equipped with multi-axis MEMS Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) present a valuable opportunity for participatory sensing~\cite{4}. Since the foundational Nericell framework~\cite{5}, extensive research has explored converting passenger vehicles into crowdsourced road surface probes~\cite{6,7,8}. However, a critical limitation pervades the existing literature: the \textbf{empirical ground-truth acquisition bottleneck}. Published studies almost universally collect training data from a single test vehicle over restricted road segments~\cite{6,7}, binding predictive models to that vehicle's mechanical transfer function $\mathcal{T}_{\text{veh}}$. Because passenger vehicle suspensions act as highly non-linear mechanical filters, a model trained on one chassis configuration exhibits systematic bias when deployed in another. Furthermore, because IMU sensors sample in the temporal domain ($f = v/\lambda$), models trained without spatial kinetic normalization suffer from velocity-induced ``staircase artifacts'' when vehicles accelerate or brake over uniform pavement~\cite{6,8}.

\textbf{Summary of Contributions.}
This paper presents a complete Sim-to-Real (Sim2Real) continuous IRI estimation architecture designed for deployment on edge consumer smartphones within a crowdsourced participatory network. Our core technical contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Curvature-Adaptive Mesh Tessellation Correction:} We identify and mathematically resolve artificial polygon-facet jitter in 3D vehicular simulators, enabling high-fidelity IRI ground truth generation across all simulated road geometries.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Vehicle, Multi-Environment Sim2Real Training Corpus:} We exploit the BeamNG.tech soft-body physics engine to generate synchronized LiDAR elevation profiles and dashboard IMU traces across 3 vehicle classes and 3 terrain environments under variable speed profiles, decoupling speed variation from reference $80\,\text{km/h}$ IRI labels.
    \item \textbf{Speed-Invariant Dual-Branch 1D-CNN with Asymmetric Huber Loss and PICA:} A lightweight dual-branch network combining depthwise-separable 1D convolutions over $100\,\text{m}$ spatial windows with inverse quadratic kinetic normalization and 2-D inverse-frequency joint histogram sampling. An Asymmetric Huber Loss and PICA correct boundary shrinkage and asymmetric severity bias.
    \item \textbf{Standards Compliance and Zero-Shot Real-World Validation:} We benchmark against 10 ML/DL baselines, evaluate against thresholds inspired by AASHTO Practice R~56 compliance tiers, and validate zero-shot domain transfer on the public PVS real-world crowdsourced dataset ($693\,\text{km}$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Standardized Pavement Roughness Measurement}
IRI was established by the World Bank in 1986~\cite{3} and standardized under ASTM~E1926 and ISO~8608. Professional evaluation relies on Inertial Profiling Systems (IPS): vehicles equipped with laser displacement sensors and high-precision inertial navigation platforms. Thresholds inspired by AASHTO Standard Practice R~56~\cite{aashto_r56} stratify systems into compliance tiers achieving sub-millimeter precision ($\pm 0.1$--$0.25\,\text{m/km}$). Their capital cost ($>\$50{,}000$--$\$200{,}000$ per vehicle) and operational complexity restrict routine surveys to primary arterial corridors, leaving residential and rural networks unmonitored for multi-year cycles.

\subsection{Smartphone-Based Participatory Sensing}
Early frameworks applied threshold heuristics to vertical accelerometer peaks to flag discrete anomalies~\cite{11,14}. Subsequent work applied statistical ML ensembles—Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees—to time-domain IMU features~\cite{6,7}. More recently, 1D-CNN and LSTM architectures have been adopted to regress continuous IRI values from multi-axis inertial time series~\cite{8}. Despite algorithmic progress, these methods suffer from \textbf{empirical field-data overfitting}: the cabin vertical acceleration $a_z(t)$ is related to the true pavement elevation profile $h(x)$ via an unknown non-linear suspension transfer function $\mathcal{T}_{\text{veh}}$, encoding chassis-specific spring and damper coefficients $c_s, k_s$. An empirical model trained on a single vehicle inadvertently learns the inverse transfer function $\mathcal{T}_{\text{veh}}^{-1}$ specific to that chassis, failing to generalize across the fleet diversity encountered in participatory deployments.

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Transfer and Mesh Tessellation Artifacts}
To overcome the empirical data scarcity bottleneck, synthetic physics simulation offers a deterministic vehicular laboratory. Soft-body physics engines such as BeamNG.tech model vehicles as fully deformable finite-element meshes, simulating tire-surface frictional contact and multi-link suspension dynamics from first principles~\cite{15}. While Sim2Real transfer is established in autonomous driving perception, to our knowledge the present work is the first to apply it to rigorous continuous pavement roughness (IRI) regression.

Exploiting 3D simulators for sub-centimeter elevation profiling introduces a non-trivial geometry challenge: \textbf{polygon-mesh tessellation artifacts}. On curved road segments, adjacent planar facets meet at dihedral angles, inducing artificial high-frequency vertical acceleration spikes concentrated in the $1$--$3\,\text{m}$ spatial wavelength band. Left uncorrected, these artifacts inflate calculated IRI to $15$--$16\,\text{m/km}$ on geometrically smooth ramps. Prior literature has not identified or corrected this mesh rendering artifact; our Curvature-Adaptive Mesh Tessellation Correction addresses this directly.

\section{Methodology: Synthetic Ground Truth Acquisition and Continuous IRI Pipeline}

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Synthetic Data Acquisition in BeamNG.tech}

\textbf{Deterministic Soft-Body Physics Laboratory.}
To eliminate empirical overfitting and obtain co-registered ground truth, we utilize BeamNG.tech (v0.38.3.0) as a deterministic vehicular physics laboratory. Unlike rigid-body simulators, BeamNG.tech models vehicles as deformable finite-element soft-body structures providing sufficient fidelity for automotive ADAS validation~\cite{15}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{Virtual sensor placement within BeamNG.tech. Dual downward-facing LiDAR sensors at left/right wheel tracks ($\pm 0.85\,\text{m}$ lateral offset) record road elevation $h(x)$; a dashboard-mounted \texttt{AdvancedIMU} records 3-axis acceleration and angular velocity.}
\label{fig:sensor_placement}
\end{figure}

We establish synchronized oracle access to pavement elevation and cabin inertial dynamics (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor_placement}) via three daemon threads: (i) a dual-beam LiDAR elevation profiler at $f_{\text{IRI}} = 100\,\text{Hz}$, recording left/right tracks $h_L(t), h_R(t)$; (ii) a dashboard IMU probe at $f_{\text{IMU}} = 100\,\text{Hz}$, replicating a smartphone in a dashboard cradle; and (iii) a forward-facing dashcam at $20\,\text{Hz}$ for contextual alignment. BeamNG's world coordinate axes are re-mapped to standard right-handed smartphone IMU conventions within the \texttt{ImuStateWorker} thread: lateral Y-world $\to$ \texttt{ax}, longitudinal X-world $\to$ \texttt{ay}, vertical Z-world $\to$ \texttt{az}, with analogous gyroscope remapping.

\textbf{Multi-Vehicle and Multi-Environment Selection.}
We selected three vehicle platforms spanning primary mechanical suspension classes: the \textbf{Roamer} (stiff body-on-frame SUV), the \textbf{Sunburst2} (balanced mid-size sedan), and the \textbf{Vivace} (soft-sprung compact hatchback). Three customized simulation environments represent diverse infrastructure: \textbf{East Coast USA} (hilly countryside with unpaved stretches), \textbf{West Coast USA} (metropolitan arterials and highway interchanges), and an \textbf{Automation Test Track} (controlled circuit with varied surface textures, used for validation).

\textbf{Variable-Speed Data Collection vs.\ Reference IRI Calculation.}
Data collection runs were executed under highly variable speed profiles, capturing rash high-speed driving, careful slow crawling ($10$--$25\,\text{km/h}$), and moderate mixed-traffic transitions. This variability affects only the cabin IMU feature matrix; IRI is a spatial geometric property of $h(x)$, invariant to collection velocity. After spatial resampling, the Golden Car model is simulated at the standardized reference velocity $v_{\text{ref}} = 80\,\text{km/h}$ ($22.22\,\text{m/s}$), ensuring labels adhere to ISO~8608 regardless of collection speed.

\subsection{The Five-Stage Continuous IRI Ground-Truth Pipeline}
Synchronized IMU and elevation traces are processed through a five-stage pipeline to produce calibrated continuous IRI ground truth (\texttt{readings\_2.csv}).

\textbf{Stage 1 — Spatial Resampling.}
Cumulative horizontal distance $l_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sqrt{(\Delta p_{x,i})^2 + (\Delta p_{y,i})^2}$ is computed from successive planar coordinate deltas. Left and right elevation traces $h_L(l), h_R(l)$ are linearly interpolated onto a uniform spatial grid at $\Delta x = 0.25\,\text{m}$, meeting ASTM~E1926 canonical sampling standards.

\textbf{Stage 2 — Curvature-Adaptive Mesh Tessellation Correction.}
On sloped or curved roads, polygon-facet dihedral angles induce artificial slope jitter ($\pm 0.3\,\text{m/m}$) in the $1$--$3\,\text{m}$ spatial wavelength band (Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh_correction}). To eliminate facet jitter while preserving real pavement anomalies, we formulate a four-step correction: (1) extract the macro design grade $z_{\text{design}}(d)$ via a $10\,\text{m}$ moving average; (2) compute local curvature $\kappa(d) = |z''_{\text{design}}(d)|$; (3) compute an anti-tessellation profile $z_{\text{smoothed}}(d)$ via a $5\,\text{m}$ moving average; (4) blend raw and smoothed profiles using
\begin{equation}
z_{\text{corrected}}(d) = (1 - \alpha(d))\,z_{\text{raw}}(d) + \alpha(d)\,z_{\text{smoothed}}(d)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha(d) = \text{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{\kappa(d) - \kappa_{\text{thresh}}}{5\kappa_{\text{thresh}}}, 0, 1\right)$ and $\kappa_{\text{thresh}} = 1.5\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^{-1}$. On flat roadways $\alpha = 0$ (raw profile preserved); on sloped flyovers $\alpha \to 1$ (facet oscillations suppressed).

\textbf{Stage 3 — Standard 250\,mm Tyre-Enveloping Filter.}
The corrected profile passes through a $250\,\text{mm}$ box moving average representing the mechanical tire contact patch, integrating micro-texture wavelengths that pneumatic tires bridge without transmitting vertical displacement to the axle.

\textbf{Stage 4 — World Bank Golden Car Quarter-Car Model.}
The enveloped profile drives the standardized World Bank Golden Car linear state-space system with canonical parameters $(c_s = 6.0\,\text{s}^{-1},\; k_s = 63.3\,\text{s}^{-2},\; k_t = 653.0\,\text{s}^{-2},\; \mu = 0.15)$ as defined by Sayers et al.~\cite{3}. The state vector $\mathbf{x}(d) = [z_s, z'_s, z_u, z'_u]^T$ collects sprung-mass and unsprung-axle displacement and slope. The state-space formulation is
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dd} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(d) + \mathbf{B}z'(d), \qquad y(d) = \mathbf{C}_{\text{out}}\mathbf{x}(d)
\end{equation}

where $y(d) = z'_s - z'_u$ is the relative suspension velocity. IRI is the normalized accumulated absolute suspension stroke over segment length $L$:
$\text{IRI} = \tfrac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|y_k|\,\Delta x \times 1000\;\text{[m/km]}$.

\textbf{Stage 5 — Spatial Segmentation and Ground-Truth Output.}
IRI is evaluated over non-overlapping $100\,\text{m}$ windows preceded by a $20\,\text{m}$ lead-in buffer allowing the quarter-car to reach steady state. Left and right wheel-track values are averaged: $\text{IRI}_{\text{avg}} = (\text{IRI}_L + \text{IRI}_R)/2$. The pipeline outputs \texttt{readings\_2.csv}, pairing each $100\,\text{m}$ cabin IMU window with its exact simulation-derived $\text{IRI}_{\text{avg}}$.

\section{Methodology: Speed-Invariant Continuous IRI Estimation Architecture}

\subsection{Smartphone IMU Preprocessing and Kinetic Normalization}
A fundamental challenge in time-domain road roughness regression is the velocity-squared scaling of vertical acceleration: a vehicle traversing a sinusoidal undulation of amplitude $A$ and spatial wavelength $\lambda$ at velocity $v$ generates peak vertical acceleration $a_{z,\text{peak}} = A(2\pi v/\lambda)^2 \propto v^2$. Consequently, a vehicle on a moderately rough road at $100\,\text{km/h}$ transmits vertical acceleration amplitudes up to $4\times$ greater than on the same road at $50\,\text{km/h}$. To decouple traversal speed from pavement roughness before feature extraction, we apply an \textbf{Inverse Quadratic Kinetic Normalization}:
\begin{equation}
a_{z,\text{norm}}(t) = a_z(t) \cdot \left( \frac{v_{\text{ref}}}{\max(v(t),\, v_{\text{safe}})} \right)^2
\end{equation}
where $v_{\text{ref}} = 22.22\,\text{m/s}$ (canonical reference speed) and $v_{\text{safe}} = 3.0\,\text{m/s}$ is a lower-bound stability clamp preventing numerical amplification jitter at near-zero speeds.

\subsection{2-D Inverse-Frequency Joint Histogram Sampling}
In observational driving data, vehicle speed and road roughness exhibit strong negative correlation ($\rho < -0.65$): drivers slow down on deteriorated roads. A network trained via standard mini-batch sampling exploits this correlation, mapping low speed to high IRI rather than interpreting structural vibrations. To enforce statistical speed invariance, we construct a 2-D joint histogram $H(b_v, b_{\text{IRI}})$ discretizing the $(v, \text{IRI})$ domain into $10 \times 10$ uniform bins. Each training sample $i$ receives a sampling weight $w_i \propto 1/\max(H(b_v(i), b_{\text{IRI}}(i)), 1)$, ensuring uniform exposure across all speed--roughness combinations.

\subsection{Dual-Branch Depthwise-Separable 1D-CNN Architecture}
We propose a lightweight Dual-Branch Depthwise-Separable 1D-CNN (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_curve}) tailored for on-device smartphone inference.

\textbf{Branch A — Temporal-Spatial Waveform Encoder.}
The normalized IMU matrix $\mathbf{X}_{\text{norm}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 6}$ enters a stack of three depthwise-separable 1D convolutional blocks (kernel sizes $k = 7, 5, 3$; filter counts $32, 64, 128$; each followed by Batch Normalization, GELU activation, and Dropout). Depthwise-separable convolutions reduce parameter count by ${\sim}80\%$ over standard 1D convolutions. The output feature map $\mathbf{Z}$ undergoes parallel Global Average Pooling and Global Max Pooling—capturing both sustained roughness energy and acute impulse events—which are concatenated into $\mathbf{f}_{\text{conv}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$.

\textbf{Branch B — Contextual Physics Summary Vector.}
In parallel, a contextual summary vector $\mathbf{s} = [\bar{v},\, \overline{|a_z|},\, \overline{|a_y|},\, \text{ZCR}(a_z)]^T \in \mathbb{R}^4$ is extracted, where $\text{ZCR}(a_z)$ is the zero-crossing rate of vertical acceleration—a proxy for suspension natural frequency excitation.

\textbf{Feature Fusion and Monotonic Output Head.}
The embeddings $\mathbf{f}_{\text{conv}}$ and $\mathbf{s}$ are concatenated into $\mathbf{f}_{\text{fused}} \in \mathbb{R}^{260}$ and passed through two dense layers (64 and 32 units, GELU activation, Dropout 0.2). The final layer applies a $\text{Softplus}(z) = \ln(1 + e^z)$ activation to guarantee strictly positive predictions ($\widehat{\text{IRI}} > 0$).

\subsection{Asymmetric Huber Loss and Post-Training Isotonic Calibration (PICA)}
Standard MSE optimization on skewed civil engineering datasets suffers from regression shrinkage toward the dataset mean (${\sim}3$--$4\,\text{m/km}$). We optimize using an \textbf{Asymmetric Huber Loss} ($\delta = 1.0$) applying a 4:1 penalty to under-predictions on high-severity segments ($\text{IRI} \geq 4.5\,\text{m/km}$):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AHuber}}(y, \hat{y}) = w_{\text{asym}}(y, \hat{y}) \cdot H_\delta(y - \hat{y})
\end{equation}
where $H_\delta(e)$ is the standard Huber loss ($\frac{1}{2}e^2$ for $|e| \le \delta$, else $\delta(|e| - \frac{1}{2}\delta)$) and $w_{\text{asym}} = 4.0$ if $y - \hat{y} > 0$ and $y \ge 4.5$, else $1.0$.
%% Stand-alone standard Huber equation inlined above per reviewer.

To eliminate residual boundary shrinkage, we apply \textbf{Post-Training Isotonic Calibration (PICA)} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pica_eval}). A monotonic step function $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is fitted on validation predictions $\{\hat{y}_i\}$ by solving the Pool-Adjacent-Violators Algorithm (PAVA):
\begin{equation}
\min_{g \in \mathcal{M}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( g(\hat{y}_i) - y_i \right)^2 \quad \text{subject to } g(u) \le g(v) \; \forall u < v
\end{equation}
PICA preserves prediction ranking while correcting systematic scale compression, yielding calibrated predictions $\widehat{\text{IRI}}_{\text{cal}} = g(\hat{y})$.

A notable physical validation emerged from inspecting predictions across complex European surfaces in the PVS dataset: our model consistently assigned higher IRI values to historical cobblestone roads ($6.2$--$7.8\,\text{m/km}$) than to rural unpaved dirt roads ($4.1$--$5.2\,\text{m/km}$), the inverse of typical subjective passenger discomfort ratings. This result is physically correct under ASTM~E1926: cobblestones contain high-frequency vertical joint displacements that continuously excite the suspension natural frequency at moderate urban speeds ($35$--$45\,\text{km/h}$), accumulating substantial cumulative stroke per kilometer, whereas drivers intuitively slow to crawl speeds ($12$--$18\,\text{km/h}$) on dirt roads, producing large lateral sway but lower cumulative vertical quarter-car stroke. Kinetic normalization ensures the model regresses standardized mechanical roughness rather than vehicle speed or subjective occupant discomfort.

\subsection{Speed-Invariance and Multi-Vehicle Generalization Stress Testing}
We evaluated the model across three vehicle classes driven over identical evaluation profiles under controlled speed steps from $13.1\,\text{km/h}$ ($3.6\,\text{m/s}$) to $109.1\,\text{km/h}$ ($30.3\,\text{m/s}$). Uncalibrated baselines exhibit a quadratic positive slope, predicting artificial roughness exceeding $12\,\text{m/km}$ at highway speeds. In contrast, our architecture maintains flat, invariant IRI predictions across the primary operational envelope ($25$--$85\,\text{km/h}$) across all three vehicle classes. Minor estimation drift at extreme crawl speeds ($<15\,\text{km/h}$) arises from low-speed signal-to-noise degradation; slight deviation at extreme highway speeds ($>95\,\text{km/h}$) corresponds to physical suspension bump-stop bottoming accurately captured by BeamNG soft-body physics.

\section{Conclusion and Municipal Deployment Roadmap}

We have presented a speed-invariant, multi-vehicle continuous IRI estimation architecture trained via a Sim-to-Real soft-body physics pipeline. By leveraging BeamNG.tech across distinct vehicle suspension classes and customized environments, we overcome the empirical single-vehicle overfitting bottleneck that has constrained participatory pavement sensing. Our Curvature-Adaptive Mesh Tessellation Correction resolves artificial polygon-facet jitter on curved slopes, enabling high-fidelity synthetic ground truth generation.

Incorporating inverse quadratic kinetic normalization, 2-D inverse-frequency joint histogram sampling, Asymmetric Huber Loss ($\delta=1.0$), and PICA, our Dual-Branch 1D-CNN achieves MAE of $1.642\,\text{m/km}$ ($R^2 = 0.493$), outperforming gradient-boosted decision trees by 19.1\% and LSTM networks by 30.9\%. Against thresholds inspired by AASHTO R~56 compliance tiers, the model reaches 63.6\% at Surveying Grade and 77.9\% at Screening Grade. Zero-shot evaluation on the PVS dataset ($693\,\text{km}$) demonstrates cross-domain transferability.

\subsection{Municipal Deployment Roadmap and Future Work}
A three-phase deployment roadmap transitions our edge-inference engine into operational practice:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 — Opportunistic Fleet Probing:} Deploy lightweight inference onto smartphones inside municipal fleet vehicles (transit buses, sanitation trucks, postal vans) traversing fixed routes daily.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 — Multi-Pass Spatial Ensemble Averaging:} Aggregate independent zero-shot IRI predictions across $N$ asynchronous vehicle passes per $100\,\text{m}$ segment; spatial ensemble averaging suppresses zero-mean telemetry noise by $1/\sqrt{N}$.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 — Sensor Fusion and Drift Mitigation:} Future work will incorporate RTK GNSS carrier-phase positioning to mitigate longitudinal odometer drift, alongside real-time suspension damper temperature compensation.
\end{enumerate}
