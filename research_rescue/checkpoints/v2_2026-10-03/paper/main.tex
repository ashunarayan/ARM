\begin{abstract}
Estimating the International Roughness Index (IRI) from the vibration of ordinary cars needs paired data: drives of the car type over road sections with surveyed profiles. Vehicle simulation can generate such pairs from the profiles alone. We test what estimators trained on simulated drives transfer to a real car, using public profilometer-referenced data from one car model (LiRA-CD, four routes, \liraKm\,km), routes held out from training and 5\,km-block bootstrap intervals. Gradient boosting trained on real drives reaches a within-route mean absolute error (MAE) of \vrGbmMaeWithin\,m/km. Trained only on a randomised half-car simulation over the measured profiles of the other routes, it ranks segments within routes as well under each of nine sensor and vehicle models (Spearman \vvRhoMin--\vvRhoMax{} vs \vrGbmRhoWithin), but so does an untrained, speed-normalised vibration RMS (\rkHalfRho). What has to be learned is the level, and the zero-shot bias ranges from \vvBiasMin{} to \vvBiasMax\,m/km: the pre-specified simulator matched it (MAE \vrSimVzeroMaeWithin\,m/km) only because half of its passes carried a phone-holder resonance, and across variants the bias follows a deficit of simulated vibration at 10--15\,Hz. A level offset from 5\,km of paired drives brought every variant to \pcCalMaeFiveMin--\pcCalMaeFiveMax\,m/km, better than training on those 5\,km alone (\vbRealOnlyFive\,m/km) in \pcCalWinsFiveMin--\pcCalWinsFiveMax{} of 20 draws; with 10\,km or more, real drives alone beat the three variants with the largest deficit. Simulated vibration also grows faster with speed than the real car's. With a short calibration, simulation can stand in for most paired drives, but how many it saves depends on how well the simulated vibration matches the car, which we could test for one car only.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Simulated drives over measured road profiles taught gradient boosting to rank the road segments of a real car as well as real drives did, under every sensor and vehicle model we tried, but an untrained vibration statistic ranks them as well; the informative test is the level.
The simulated level was not reliable: it depended on the simulated vibration at 10--15\,Hz, and the simulated vibration grew faster with speed than the real car's.
Combined with a level offset from 5\,km of paired drives, simulation over all profiled roads was more accurate than training on those 5\,km alone for every simulator variant; with 10\,km or more of paired drives its advantage depended on the variant.
For a new car type this suggests simulating over roads that are already profiled, calibrating the level with a few kilometres of paired drives and checking the simulated against the real vibration spectrum before relying on the simulator.
Whether these findings hold for other vehicles, sensors and road networks requires new paired data, ideally collected after freezing the simulator.
